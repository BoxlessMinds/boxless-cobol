The Y2K Bunker

Some people stocked up on canned food. Programmers stocked up on coffee. This is the story of two digits, one millennium and an awful lot of overtime.

Two Digits

A sensible decision, made in about 1970

Storage was very expensive. If you have millions of records, each with several dates, saving two characters per date adds up. So years were stored as PIC 99: "72" meant 1972. Everyone assumed the programs would be replaced long before 2000.

They were not replaced. They were too useful.

What could go wrong?

The problem, in one line

On 1 January 2000, a two-digit year goes from 99 to 00. Any sum that subtracts years, sorts dates or checks expiry dates suddenly thinks it's 1900. Interest, pensions, bills and licences could all come out wrong.

The fixes

FixHowTrade-off
ExpansionChange the field to PIC 9(4) and store 2000, not 00. The right fix, but every file, record and report that holds the date must change too.
WindowingKeep PIC 99, but decide: below 50 means 20xx, 50 and up means 19xx. Quick and cheap. It just moves the problem to a later year.

The Aftermath

1 January 2000

Planes did not fall out of the sky. Banks opened on Monday. Most people decided Y2K had been a fuss about nothing.

Nothing happened because they fixed it. Thousands of programmers spent years reading old COBOL line by line, finding every date and changing it. It may be the most successful project nobody remembers.
