Gallery 1 · 1959–1960

Hall of Origins

In the late 1950s every computer maker had its own way of programming. A business that bought a new machine often had to rewrite everything. This gallery is about the people who decided that was silly.

The Meeting

The Pentagon, Washington DC · May 1959

Computer makers, large businesses and the US government met to talk about one shared language for business programs. One that read like English and worked on many brands of computer. The group that came out of it was called CODASYL, the Conference on Data Systems Languages.

Tell me more

The idea is often credited to Mary Hawes of Burroughs, who in early 1959 suggested that users and makers work on a common business language together. The Department of Defense agreed to host and back the effort, which gave it the weight to get competing companies into one room.

A "Short-Range Committee" was given a few months to produce something usable. Its specification became COBOL 60.

Grace Hopper

Mathematician · US Navy officer · 1906–1992

Hopper believed programs should be written in words people already knew, not in symbols only mathematicians could read. Her team's language FLOW-MATIC did exactly that, and it was one of the main models for COBOL. She advised the committee and spent decades telling anyone who would listen that computers should work for people.

Tell me more

Hopper is sometimes called the "grandmother of COBOL". She did not write COBOL alone (nobody did), but FLOW-MATIC proved that English-like programming could work on real business problems.

She retired from the Navy as a Rear Admiral and was famous for handing out pieces of wire about 30 cm long: the distance light travels in a nanosecond.

Side exhibit: The First Actual Bug

Harvard Mark II · 9 September 1947

Operators working on the Harvard Mark II found a moth stuck in a relay. They taped it into the logbook with the note "First actual case of bug being found." Hopper was on the team and loved telling the story. Engineers already said "bug" before this. The moth just made it official.

Tell me more

The real logbook page, moth included, is in the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History. It is one of the few museum objects that is also a joke.

Jean Sammet and the committee

Short-Range Committee · 1959

COBOL was designed by a committee of people from rival companies and government agencies. Jean Sammet, then at Sylvania, was one of its key members and later became the language's best-known historian. There is no single inventor of COBOL, and that was the point: nobody owned it, so everybody could use it.

Tell me more

Sammet went on to lead programming language work at IBM, became the first woman president of the ACM, and wrote Programming Languages: History and Fundamentals (1969). Her paper "The Early History of COBOL" is listed in the Reading Room.

The Two-Machine Demo

December 1960

The same COBOL program was compiled and run on two computers from different makers: an RCA 501 and a Remington Rand UNIVAC II. It gave the same results on both. Today we call that portability. In 1960 it was close to a magic trick.
